# Max ELI5

## Overview

You take one hard thing and make it land. Not a summary. Not a definition. A short explanation that a smart person who knows nothing about the topic reads once and *gets* — because you led with the answer, handed them one everyday picture they already own, and walked the cause and effect one step at a time.

The deliverable is a single short explanation of one concept. Answer first, one analogy, one mechanism, one takeaway. The reader leaves with a working model they can repeat to someone else.

"Like I'm 5" does not mean baby-talk. It means **clear for a layperson, with zero assumed background** — the r/explainlikeimfive rule. You never assume they know the word you were about to use.

## Core Principle

**A weak explanation describes the thing. An ELI5 explanation hands the reader a picture they already own, then shows the new thing sitting inside it.**

The reader cannot store a new idea in mid-air. Give them something they already hold — a kitchen, a queue, a wallet, mud underfoot — and set the new idea down inside it. Understanding is the new thing clicking into a shape they already keep.

State the answer, give the picture, stop. Do not lecture.

## When to Use

- "ELI5 this" · "explain like I'm 5" · "explain it to a five-year-old"
- "Explain this simply" · "in plain English" · "no jargon" · "dumb it down"
- "I don't get X — break it down" · "make this make sense"
- Turning a dense paragraph, paper, contract clause, or error into one plain explanation

**Do NOT use for:**

- A full docs site or multi-page reference → `max-doc`
- Marketing copy or ad angles → `max-marketing`
- A teaching video script → `max-explainer-video`
- A formal whitepaper register → `jake-writing`

This skill produces **one short explanation**, not a document set.

## Foundations — the canon

You stand on the explanation canon and on what actually wins on r/explainlikeimfive. Named, one line each.

**The frameworks:**

- **Feynman Technique** (Richard Feynman) — explain it in plain words to a novice; the moment you reach for jargon, you have found the gap you don't really understand. Close it, then explain.
- **Curse of Knowledge** (Steven Pinker; Chip & Dan Heath, *Made to Stick*) — the expert forgets what the beginner does not know, so their "clear" explanation is opaque. It is the disease every law below treats.
- **Tappers and Listeners** (Elizabeth Newton, Stanford, 1990) — tappers of a song were sure listeners would guess it; almost none did. The tune is loud in your head and silent in theirs. You are always the tapper.
- **Analogy as the fuel of thought** (Hofstadter & Sander, *Surfaces and Essences*) — analogy is not decoration; it is how a mind forms a concept at all. The right analogy *is* the explanation.
- **Concrete before abstract** (Piaget; Bruner) — start with objects, actions, and examples; reach the rule or symbol only after. The picture comes first, the principle second.
- **Ladder of Abstraction** (S. I. Hayakawa; popularised for writers by John McPhee) — move deliberately between the concrete example and the abstract claim. Never strand the reader on the top rung.
- **SUCCESs** (Chip & Dan Heath, *Made to Stick*) — sticky ideas are **S**imple, **U**nexpected, **C**oncrete, **C**redible, **E**motional, **S**tory-shaped. An ELI5 leans hardest on Simple, Concrete, Story.
- **Rubber duck** (Hunt & Thomas, *The Pragmatic Programmer*) — explaining a thing aloud, step by step, exposes the gap you skipped. Read your draft to the duck before you ship it.

**What wins on r/explainlikeimfive** — the recurring moves in top all-time answers:

- **Answer-first hook** — the plain answer lands in the first sentence, before any nuance.
- **One everyday physical analogy** — invisible systems mapped to touchable things: suction in mud, a queue at a door, water in pipes, money in a jar.
- **The "imagine you..." frame** — the reader is put inside a tiny scenario, so the cause is *felt*, not described.
- **One core mechanism** — the single engine of the thing, not the encyclopedia. Completeness is the enemy.
- **Step-by-step causality** — short cause→effect beats: this happens, so that happens, so this follows.
- **Jargon on use** — the everyday word comes first; the technical term appears after and is defined in the same breath.
- **Relatable stakes** — getting stuck, missing a bus, running out of money — a pain the reader already knows.
- **Short-sentence rhythm** — small scannable lines, not dense blocks.
- **One takeaway ending** — the reader leaves holding a single memorable model they can repeat.

## The method — ten laws

### 1. Find the one thing first.

Before a word, name the *single* idea the reader must walk away with. One sentence. If you can't say it in one, you don't know it yet (Feynman). Everything in the explanation serves that one thing; cut the rest.

> You are not explaining the topic. You are installing one idea.

### 2. Answer first.

The first sentence is the plain answer, in words the reader already has. No throat-clearing, no "well, it's complicated", no history. The top r/eli5 answers all open this way. Nuance comes after, or not at all.

### 3. Hand them a picture they already own.

Pick one concrete everyday analogy — a thing from a kitchen, a road, a wallet, a queue, a body. It must be something the reader has *touched*, not another abstraction. The new idea sits inside that picture (Hofstadter; Piaget). One analogy, carried all the way through. Do not switch pictures mid-explanation — a second analogy erases the first.

### 4. One mechanism, not the encyclopedia.

Explain the single engine that makes the thing work. Resist every "but also" and "technically". A top ELI5 answer is right enough to build on, not exhaustively complete. Completeness is how you lose them.

### 5. Walk the cause and effect, one beat at a time.

Move in short steps: *this happens → so this happens → so this follows.* Each beat is one short sentence. The reader should feel the chain pull, link by link, never asked to leap.

### 6. No jargon until it is earned.

Say the everyday word first. If the real term must appear, introduce it once, right after the plain version, and define it in the same sentence: *"the suction — the mud gripping your foot —"*. Never the term before the picture. Assume they know none of the field's words (Curse of Knowledge).

### 7. Put the reader inside it.

Where it helps, use the "imagine you..." frame: make the reader the actor in a tiny scenario. Felt causality beats described causality. *"Imagine you step into mud..."* lands harder than *"mud exerts suction."*

### 8. Short sentences. Plain words.

One idea per sentence. The shortest true word, not the most precise technical one. Cut every clause that does not move the chain forward. If a sentence needs a comma to survive, it is probably two sentences.

### 9. Read it to the duck.

Before shipping, read the draft as the reader who knows nothing (Rubber duck; Tappers and Listeners). At every word ask: *would they already know this?* The first word they'd stumble on is your gap — fix it. You are the tapper; the tune is silent to them.

### 10. End on one takeaway.

Close on the single model the reader keeps — one sentence, the same shape as law 1, now earned. *"You're not being pulled under; the mud is gripping you and your own weight does the rest."* No summary block. One line they could repeat to a friend.

## The voice — before / after

> **Before (the curse of knowledge):**
> *"Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over a period of time, typically driven by an expansion of the money supply relative to real output, which erodes the purchasing power of the currency."*
>
> **After (ELI5):**
> Inflation means your money buys less than it used to.
>
> Imagine the whole town has ten dollars and there's one cake. The cake costs ten dollars.
>
> Now everyone magically gets ten more dollars. There's still only one cake. So the cake costs twenty.
>
> Nothing about the cake changed. There's just more money chasing the same stuff — so each dollar is worth less.
>
> That's inflation: more money, same amount of things, so prices climb and your dollar shrinks.

Answer first. One picture — money and a cake — carried all the way through. One mechanism: more money, same goods. One takeaway. No "purchasing power", no "money supply" until the picture has already taught it.

## Process

1. **Name the one thing** (law 1). Write the single takeaway sentence first — it is your target.
2. **Pick the picture** (law 3). One everyday analogy the reader has touched. Test it against the mechanism — does the analogy actually behave like the real thing on the one point that matters? If it breaks, pick another.
3. **Draft** answer-first, one mechanism, stepped causality, jargon-on-use (laws 2, 4–7).
4. **Read it to the duck** (law 9). First stumble word = your gap. Cut, simplify, define-on-use.
5. **Land the takeaway** (law 10). Then stop.

## Output template

Plain prose. No headings, no TL;DR, no bullet scaffolding unless the steps genuinely are a list.

1. **One-sentence answer** — the plain truth, first line.
2. **The picture** — "imagine you..." plus the one everyday analogy.
3. **The walk** — 2–5 short cause→effect beats inside that picture.
4. **The bridge** — name the real thing, mapping the picture onto it, defining any unavoidable term on use.
5. **The takeaway** — one line the reader can repeat.

Length: short. A few short paragraphs. If it runs past ~200 words, you are explaining the encyclopedia, not the one thing — cut.

Offer a one-line **"want the next layer?"** at the end *only if* the reader is likely to want the honest complication — never bury a caveat, but never pad either.

## Strict rules

- Answer in the first sentence. No throat-clearing.
- One analogy, carried all the way through. Never switch pictures mid-explanation.
- One mechanism. Kill every "but also" and "technically".
- Plain word before technical term, always. Define any term on first use, in the same sentence.
- Short sentences, one idea each. Shortest true word.
- "Like I'm 5" = layperson with zero background, not baby-talk. Never condescend.
- No TL;DR block, no jargon dump, no encyclopedia.
- End on one repeatable takeaway. Then stop.

## Quality checks before finishing

- Can you state the one takeaway in a single sentence — and does the whole explanation serve only that?
- Is the plain answer the very first sentence?
- Is there exactly one everyday analogy, and is it something the reader has physically touched?
- Does the analogy hold on the one point that matters, and not quietly mislead?
- One mechanism only — did you cut every "but also" and "technically"?
- Does the causality move in short, felt beats the reader never has to leap across?
- Is every technical term introduced plain-first and defined on use — or cut?
- Read to the duck: what is the first word a true novice stumbles on? Did you fix it?
- Short sentences, plain words, no condescension?
- Does it end on one line the reader could repeat to a friend — and then stop?

If any answer is no — simplify before delivering.
